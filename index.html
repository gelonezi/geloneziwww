02 / Featured system

Replacing a legacy monolith, one operation at a time

The integration platform I architected at Softplan. Internal products needed to talk to ~20 court and prosecutor systems that each spoke a different dialect and changed without notice. I gave them one API contract, built as ~25 .NET microservices on AWS EKS, that replaced the legacy platform over four years without a single incident at cutover.

  • ~25.NET microservices on AWS EKS
  • 200k+requests per day
  • ~20judicial & prosecutor systems integrated
  • 0incidents across client cutovers
DWG-01 · Integration platform · reference view Select a component · scroll sideways →

Component

The whole platform

Consumers integrate once. Behind that single contract, stateless connectors absorb the constant change in external court systems, and each one ships, scales and fails independently.

  • Vertical Slice
  • Hexagonal
  • MassTransit
  • Saga

Design decisions

  1. N-01

    One contract, many systems

    Consumers integrate once, against a five-operation API. Churn in external systems stays behind it.

  2. N-02

    Stateless connectors

    One adapter per external system, deployed and scaled independently, so one slow court never drags down the rest.

  3. N-03

    Shared contracts in an SDK

    Protocol versions (a national SOAP standard, plus REST) and per-system corner cases live in one versioned package.

  4. N-04

    Configuration over code

    A catalog describes each external system, so onboarding a new court starts as configuration.

  5. N-05

    Sync and async paths

    Request/response through the ingress; long-running work through RabbitMQ, with multi-step workflows as MassTransit sagas.

  6. N-06

    Replace, observe, retire

    Vertical slices replaced legacy operations one by one. Elastic APM traces were the evidence behind each client cutover.

05 / Expertise in depth

Modernize, integrate, operate

Most of my work happens after the greenfield phase: inside systems that are already running a business.

E-01 · Legacy modernization

Modernizing systems without stopping the business

Put a contract in front of the old system, replace it piece by piece, and prove each step in production before retiring anything. Analysis and documentation come first; big-bang rewrites don't.

  • Softplan: a judiciary integration platform replaced operation by operation over ~4 years, client by client, with zero-incident cutovers and final legacy retirement.
  • Eris Sistemas: analyzed ~60 functional areas of a VB6 + .NET Framework platform and planned its move to C#/.NET Core, delivering the first domain.
  • Earlier: Visual Basic to C#/.NET at Solução Informática; spreadsheet workflows to a web application at MODEC.
  1. Understand legacy behavior and business rules
  2. Map dependencies and migration risks
  3. Define the replacement contract
  4. Migrate incrementally, one operation at a time
  5. Run old and new side by side during the transition
  6. Validate behavior and monitor production
  7. Cut over client by client, with rollback in mind
  8. Document, then retire the legacy component

06 / How I work

Five principles I ship by

  • P-01

    Agents draft, I decide

    AI tools inside guardrails: specs first, small diffs, tests, and every change reviewed before merge. The architecture and the accountability stay mine.

  • P-02

    Decisions in writing

    RFCs and ADRs turn hallway decisions into durable context. New engineers learn why, not just what, and architecture stops depending on who was in the room.

  • P-03

    Observable, or it isn't done

    Metrics, traces and alerts ship with the feature. That's what turned zero-incident cutovers into a measurement instead of a hope.

  • P-04

    Build teams that outgrow me

    Mentoring from intern to senior, onboarding that sticks, and knowledge transfer deep enough that a client's own team can take over the codebase.

  • P-05

    Own the outcome, not the ticket

    Architecture, implementation, deployment and production behavior are one engineering problem. I'd rather own it end to end than hand work off at the edge of a ticket.

Technical leadership

Leverage, not heroics

Staff-level work is mostly decisions other people can build on, and engineers who don't need me in the room. I stay hands-on while doing it.
